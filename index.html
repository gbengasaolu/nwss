What is the WVAL?

Imagine you are checking the weather. A thermometer gives you one number that tells you how hot it is. The Wastewater Viral Activity Level, or WVAL, is like a thermometer for germs in a community.

Every time we flush a toilet or use a sink, the water goes down the drain and travels through pipes to a treatment plant. Sick people sometimes leave tiny bits of germs in that water. Scientists take a small sample of the water and measure how much of a virus is in it. The WVAL turns that measurement into an easy score.

The big idea: The WVAL compares the amount of a virus right now to the usual low level of that virus at the same place during the last 24 months (2 years).

Why a "score" and not just a count?

Different cities are different sizes and use different pipes. If we just compared raw amounts, a big city and a small town could never be compared fairly. The WVAL puts every place on the same ruler, so the number means the same thing everywhere. This is called standardizing.

Why is it important?

For you and your family

If you see the WVAL go up, there may be a higher risk of getting sick in your area. That is a helpful clue! You can use it to make smart choices to protect yourself and the people around you.

Some common ways people protect themselves from respiratory viruses (these are general tips, so follow advice from your own doctor and health department): stay up to date on recommended vaccines, wash hands often, stay home when sick, and think about extra care around babies, older adults, and people with weaker immune systems when levels are higher.

For public health teams

Health officials use wastewater (sewage) data together with clinical data (like doctor and hospital information) to understand how much virus is in a community. The WVAL helps them to:

How is the WVAL made?

There are six steps after a quick "what's needed" check. Walk through them one at a time. Each step has something to try.

Before we start: enough data

For every site, we need 8 weeks of data that were measured with the same lab method. Having too little data can make numbers jump around wildly. Eight weeks keeps things steady.

Try it: Is this site ready?

Step 1: Group data together

To compare apples with apples, we put samples into groups. Each group has the same: (1) virus, (2) site, and (3) lab and lab method.

Try it: Who belongs in the same group?

Our group is COVID-19 · Riverton · Lab 1. Tap every sample that belongs, then press Check.

Step 2: Validate (clean and calm) the data

Two tricks make the data ready for fair comparison.

Trick A: Log transform. Virus amounts can differ hugely, like 10 versus 1,000,000. A log transform shrinks giant gaps so we can see the overall pattern clearly.

Try it: Squish the giants

Trick B: Limit unusually high values. Extremely high amounts are capped at the 99th percentile of similarly grouped data. This is called Winsorization. The big number stays in the data, but it can no longer push the answer around.

Try it: Throw away or cap?

Step 3: Find the "typical low level" for each site

Look at the virus amounts from the past 2 years at that site. Pick the value that is higher than 10% of all the values. This is the site's baseline, its typical low level. Today's amount will be compared to it.

New sites: They do not have 2 years of history yet. While a site becomes established, an adapted lab-based baseline and standard deviation (made from all the data at the site's lab) are used: 365 days for influenza A and RSV, and 182 days for SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19). This gives steadier numbers.

Reminder: When baselines are refreshed

Tap a pin. Baselines are recalculated when the virus is usually low, so today's levels are always compared to low levels. All historical WVAL values are updated with the new baseline.

Tap a pin above.

Step 4: Compare today to the baseline

Find how far today's amount is above the baseline, then divide by the standard deviation (a measure of how much the site's numbers usually wiggle). The result is a standardized score: how many "wiggle-sizes" above the typical low level we are.

WVAL = current − baselinestandard deviation

Try it with pretend numbers

Higher WVAL = farther above the typical low level.

Because every site is measured in "wiggle-sizes" from its own baseline, scores can be compared across sites.

Step 5: Average each week

For each week, we average the WVAL values for each site. Averages smooth out single odd days and show the weekly trend.

Try it: Make a weekly average

Step 6: Sort into five categories

Finally, each WVAL goes into one of five groups: very low, low, moderate, high, very high. This gives a quick, easy picture.

Important: Each virus has its own cut-offs, because each virus behaves differently. A WVAL of 10.0 is moderate for influenza A, high for COVID-19, and very high for RSV.

Grown-up science box: why these choices?

Log transform: viral concentrations in wastewater span several orders of magnitude and are roughly log-normally distributed; the log scale stabilizes variance. Winsorization keeps all observations while bounding leverage from extreme values. Baseline at the 10th percentile over 24 months approximates a "low-activity" reference. Standardizing by the standard deviation expresses the departure in site-specific units so sites with different assays and dilution can be compared. Each virus has distinct thresholds because the distribution of WVAL values differs among viruses.

Which sites are included?

New sites, and sites that make a big change to their lab method, must wait until they have at least 8 weeks of reported samples before they join the medians. Influenza A and RSV have one extra rule: sites must start sampling by October 1 each year for their data to be displayed that season.

COVID-19
Included in national, regional, state or territorial medians after 8 weeks.
Influenza A and RSV
Included in national and state or territorial medians after 8 weeks, and must begin sampling by Oct 1.

6

Keeping it fresh: yearly updates

Wastewater monitoring is a growing science. Each year, CDC works with wastewater scientists at state and local health departments and with infectious disease experts to check and improve the methods. This is like regular tune-ups for a car.

  • The review happens each summer, before the fall/winter respiratory season. New methods are published and applied each August.
  • Baselines use the latest two years of data, so WVAL ranges can shift as new data replace old.
  • CDC checks the methods against clinical data from the National Syndromic Surveillance Program (NSSP).
  • When methods change, they are applied to all historical data, not only new data. That way, differences you see over time come from the virus, not from changes in math.

What changed in August 2026?

Open each box to see "before" and "now".

1. Outliers (extreme values)
Before
Values with a z-score greater than four were removed.
Now
Extreme values are Winsorized at the 99th percentile: included, but their impact is limited.
2. Baselines and standard deviations
Before
New sites had too little data, so scores could jump to extremes.
Now
New sites borrow a baseline and standard deviation from all data at their lab for the first 182 days (COVID-19) or 365 days (influenza A, RSV). After that, they use their own data. This is steadier and avoids extreme scores caused by limited data.
3. Aggregation (combining)
Before
The national WVAL was the median of the regional WVALs.
Now
The national WVAL is the median of the jurisdictional (state/territory) WVALs. This is more consistent across programs that define regions differently, and it makes the link between state and national numbers clearer.
4. WVAL thresholds (the category cut-offs)
Before
Influenza A and RSV used Jenks Natural Breaks; COVID-19 used percentiles.
Now
All three use Jenks Natural Breaks on the latest 24 months. Performance was checked against NSSP categories. Small updates keep the thresholds in line with recent activity.
Fun fact Jenks Natural Breaks is a method that looks for the natural gaps in a set of numbers and puts the cut-offs there, like sorting a pile of crayons into groups that look alike.

Reading the WVAL like a pro

Remember the three clues: current level, trend, and other data. Put them together before you decide what to do. Try these real-life situations (all pretend places).

Things the WVAL can and cannot tell you

It can
  • Show how far virus levels are above a typical low level
  • Compare places and times fairly
  • Show rising or falling trends
  • Give an early view of community-wide changes
Keep in mind
  • It is a community measure, not a test of one person
  • A state median may hide hot spots in some places
  • Categories differ for each virus
  • Past values can change slightly after yearly updates

Word helper

Wastewater: used water from homes and buildings that goes to a treatment plant.
Site: a place where wastewater samples are collected.
Viral concentration: how much virus is in a sample.
Baseline: the typical low level for a site.
Standard deviation: how much numbers usually wiggle around.
Median: the middle value in a sorted list.
Percentile: the 99th percentile is higher than 99 out of 100 values.
Log transform: a math step that shrinks very big gaps.
Winsorization: capping extreme values instead of removing them.
Clinical data: information from health care, like doctor visits.
Jurisdiction: a state, territory, or similar reporting area.
Recalibration: a yearly tune-up of the methods and cut-offs.
